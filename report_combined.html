Problem P1: Factory Production Speed (Schaffer Convex Bi-Objective)

1. Standard Mathematical Formulation

\[\min_{x} \quad f(x) = \begin{bmatrix} f_1(x) \\ f_2(x) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (x - 3)^2 \\ (x - 7)^2 \end{bmatrix}\]

\[\text{subject to} \quad 1.0 \le x \le 10.0\]

  • Objectives: Conflicting quadratic trade-off between machine operational speed and tool maintenance wear.
  • Pareto Optimal Set: \(x^* \in [3.0, 7.0]\).
  • Analytical Pareto Front: \(f_2 = (4 - \sqrt{f_1})^2\) for \(f_1 \in [0, 16]\), strictly convex.

2. Decision Variable Encoding

  • Dimension: \(D = 1\) continuous scalar.
  • Bounds: \(x_l = 1.0, x_u = 10.0\).
  • Representation: Double-precision floating point.

3. Constraint Handling

  • Bound constraints handled via box clamping: \(x = \text{clip}(x, 1.0, 10.0)\).
  • No interior constraints (\(CV(x) = 0.0\)).

4. Objective Normalization & Scaling

  • Objective scales are balanced (\(f_1, f_2 \in [0, 49]\) over the full domain; \(f_1, f_2 \in [0, 16]\) along the Pareto front).
  • Normalization: \(f_{norm} = (f - z^) / (z^{nad} - z^)\) where \(z^* = [0, 0]\) and \(z^{nad} = [16, 16]\).

5. Hypervolume Reference Point

  • Reference Point: \(r = [18.0, 18.0]\) (unnormalized), representing a 12.5% offset beyond the nadir point \([16.0, 16.0]\).
  • Normalized reference point: \(r_{norm} = [1.1, 1.1]\).

6. Termination Criteria & Computational Budget

  • Budget: 3,750 function evaluations (25 generations \(\times\) 150 population size) for evolutionary metaheuristics; tolerance \(\text{ftol} = 10^{-6}\) or max 100 iterations for classical mathematical solvers.

Comparative Family Analysis: How Algorithmic Families Differ

Across the 37 algorithms investigated in this laboratory catalogue, the structural mechanisms for managing diversity, convergence, constraints, and non-convex Pareto fronts differ fundamentally across algorithmic families:

  • Diversity Handling:
  • Classical methods (1–7) achieve diversity solely through extrinsic parameter sweeps (varying weights, \(\varepsilon\) bounds, or goals). They have no built-in awareness of point spread.
  • Non-elitist evolutionary methods (10–13) rely on artificial phenotypic or genotypic fitness sharing (\(\sigma_{share}\)), which requires manual radius tuning and suffers from genetic drift.
  • Elitist algorithms (14, 16, 17) introduce parameterless intrinsic diversity operators: NSGA-II uses crowding distance in objective space, SPEA2 uses \(k\)-th nearest neighbor density, and PESA-II introduces region-based hyperbox selection.
  • Many-objective algorithms (15, 31, 34) replace crowding distance with structured reference vectors (NSGA-III, RVEA) or Monte Carlo sample sharing (HypE), because in high dimensions (\(M \ge 5\)) all solutions become non-dominated and crowding distance loses discrimination.
  • Convergence Mechanics:
  • Classical single-objective scalarizers converge rapidly to high numerical precision using deterministic gradient or quadratic solvers, but each solve yields only one point.
  • Swarm metaheuristics (22, 23, 24, 29) achieve very rapid early convergence driven by global attractor leaders (\(\alpha\)-wolf, gbest, teacher, guide bat), but risk premature stagnation.
  • Evolutionary algorithms (14, 16, 19, 27) achieve robust, population-wide asymptotic convergence via recombination and elitist archives. Differential evolution (MOEA/D-DE, MODE) demonstrates the fastest convergence among evolutionary operators.
  • Gradient-based methods (36, 37) leverage exact derivative information to achieve linear or superlinear convergence along common descent directions, terminating rigorously at Pareto-critical KKT stationary points.
  • Constraint Handling:
  • Deb's feasibility-first rules (21) dominate modern evolutionary constraint handling by enforcing three parameter-free rules: feasible beats infeasible, lower constraint violation beats higher violation, and Pareto dominance breaks ties among feasible solutions. This avoids penalty parameter tuning entirely.
  • Classical methods handle constraints natively via mathematical programming (SLSQP, active set, barrier methods).
  • Swarm and physics metaheuristics without Deb's rules must rely on exterior penalty functions, which distort the landscape gradient when penalty coefficients are poorly calibrated.
  • Handling Non-Convex (Dented) Fronts:
  • Weighted Sum (1) fails completely on non-convex Pareto fronts (such as ZDT2, P3): supporting hyperplanes roll off dented regions, causing solutions to jump exclusively between extreme ends.
  • \(\varepsilon\)-Constraint (2) and Tchebycheff Weight Metric (4) overcome this limitation by cutting the objective space with orthogonal bounds, successfully reaching every point on non-convex frontiers.
  • Pareto-dominance and indicator-based algorithms (14, 17, 32, 33) naturally capture non-convex fronts because Pareto dominance and hypervolume are invariant to curvature shape.

2. Deep Dive: Algorithm 17 — PESA-II

2.1 Study Card

  • Full Form: Pareto Envelope-based Selection Algorithm II.
  • Year & Authors: David W. Corne, Nick R. Jerram, Joshua D. Knowles, & Martin J. Oates (GECCO 2001).
  • Problem It Was Invented to Fix: Eliminates the crowding bias inherent in individual-based selection, where crowded clusters are sampled disproportionately often, destroying front diversity.
  • Core Idea (<= 5 Lines):

> Replaces individual selection with Region-Based Selection on an external hyperbox grid: binary tournament selection chooses between two non-empty hyperboxes (the box with the smaller squeeze factor wins), and then picks a single individual uniformly from the winning box. This ensures sparse regions are sampled just as frequently as dense regions.

flowchart TD
    A["Discretize Objective Space into K^M Hyperboxes"] --> B["Map Archive Members into Grid Boxes & Count Squeeze Factor"]
    B --> C["Region Tournament: Pick 2 Non-Empty Boxes; Lower Squeeze Factor Wins"]
    C --> D["Uniformly Pick One Parent from Winning Box"]
    D --> E["Perform SBX Crossover & Polynomial Mutation to Create Offspring"]
    E --> F["Update Archive: Replace Dominated Members; Prune Most Crowded Box if Full"]

\[\text{box}_m(x) = \min\left(K - 1, \max\left(0, \left\lfloor K \cdot \frac{f_m(x) - f_m^{\min}}{f_m^{\max} - f_m^{\min} + \varepsilon}\right\rfloor\right)\right)\]

Hyperbox coordinate: \(B(x) = (\text{box}_1(x), \dots, \text{box}_M(x))\).

  • Squeeze Factor Calculation:
  • Region-Based Tournament Selection:

Sample two non-empty boxes \(B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}\).

\[B^* = \begin{cases} B_1 & \text{if } \text{sq}(B_1) < \text{sq}(B_2) \\ B_2 & \text{if } \text{sq}(B_2) < \text{sq}(B_1) \\ \text{Random}(B_1, B_2) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\]

Sample Parent \(P_1 \sim \text{Uniform}(\{x \in \text{Archive} : B(x) = B^*\})\). Repeat for Parent \(P_2\).

  • Recombination & Mutation:

Produce offspring \(c\) via SBX crossover (\(\eta_c = 20\)) and polynomial mutation (\(\eta_m = 20\)).

  • Archive Insertion & Capacity Pruning:

If \(c\) is non-dominated by Archive:

  • Remove all archive members dominated by \(c\).
$$\text{sq}(B) = \{x \in \text{Archive} : B(x) = B\} $$
- If $ \text{Archive} > N_{arc}\(, find box \)B_{\text{max}} = \arg\max \text{sq}(B)\( and delete one random individual from \)B_{\text{max}}$.

  • Scaling (\(M > 3\)): Number of possible hyperboxes scales as \(K^M\), but since only non-empty boxes are stored in a hash map, space complexity is bounded by \(O(N_{arc})\).
  • Time Complexity: \(O(M \cdot N_{arc})\) per offspring generation.

3. Deep Dive: Algorithm 29 — MOBA (Multi-Objective Bat Algorithm)

3.1 Study Card

  • Full Form: Multi-Objective Bat Algorithm.
  • Year & Author: Xin-She Yang (2011, Int. Journal of Bio-Inspired Computation).
  • Problem It Was Invented to Fix: Incorporates multi-frequency echolocation tuning and dynamic pulse emission rates to automatically balance global exploration with localized exploitation in multi-objective landscapes.
  • Core Idea (<= 5 Lines):

> Emulates microbats foraging via echolocation: each bat adjusts its pulse frequency and velocity towards a guide solution \(x_*\) drawn from an external Pareto archive. When pulse rate criteria are met, bats perform local random walks scaled by acoustic loudness \(A\). Successful moves decay loudness and elevate pulse rate \(r\), transitioning the swarm from broad exploration to intense local refinement.

flowchart TD
    A["Initialize Bats: Positions x_i, Velocities v_i=0, Loudness A_i, Pulse Rate r_i"] --> B["Select Guide x* from External Pareto Archive via Crowding"]
    B --> C["Update Frequency f_i, Velocity v_i, and Position x_i"]
    C --> D{"rand > r_i ?"}
    D -- Yes --> E["Perform Local Random Walk: x_cand = x* + eps * A_mean"]
    D -- No --> F["Retain Global Position: x_cand = x_i"]
    E --> G["Evaluate Objectives & Check Constraint Feasibility"]
    F --> G
    G --> H{"x_cand Dominates or rand < A_i ?"}
    H -- Yes --> I["Accept: x_i = x_cand; Decay Loudness A_i = alpha*A_i; Elevate Pulse Rate r_i"]
    H -- No --> J["Reject Position Update"]
    I --> K["Update External Pareto Archive & Prune"]
    J --> K

Numbered Steps with Formulas

  • Frequency & Velocity Update:

\[f_i = f_{\min} + (f_{\max} - f_{\min}) \beta, \quad \beta \sim U(0, 1)\]

\[v_i^{(t+1)} = v_i^{(t)} + (x_i^{(t)} - x_*) f_i\]

\[x_i^{(t+1)} = x_i^{(t)} + v_i^{(t+1)}\]

where \(x_*\) is selected from the non-dominated archive via crowding tournament.

  • Local Echolocation Walk:

If \(\text{rand}() > r_i^{(t)}\):

\[x_{\text{cand}} = x_* + \varepsilon \cdot \bar{A}^{(t)} \cdot (x_u - x_l)\]

where \(\varepsilon \sim U(-1, 1)\) and \(\bar{A}^{(t)} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N A_i^{(t)}\).

  • Acceptance & Emission Schedules:

If \(x_{\text{cand}}\) dominates \(x_i\) or with probability \(A_i\):

\[x_i^{(t+1)} = x_{\text{cand}}\]

\[A_i^{(t+1)} = \alpha A_i^{(t)}, \quad 0 < \alpha < 1\]

\[r_i^{(t+1)} = r_i^{(0)} [1 - \exp(-\gamma t)], \quad \gamma > 0\]

  • Archive Pruning: Maintain fixed capacity \(N_{arc}\) using crowding distance truncation.

Parameter Sensitivity

  • \(\alpha\) (Loudness cooling factor):
  • Too small (\(\alpha = 0.70\)): Loudness vanishes immediately; local search freezes prematurely, causing convergence stagnation.
  • Too large (\(\alpha = 0.99\)): Loudness remains high; bats continue taking large random steps late in the run, preventing fine convergence.
  • \(\gamma\) (Pulse rate acceleration):
  • Too large: Pulse emission rate \(r_i \to 1\) too quickly, locking out local random walks.

Performance Analysis

  • Convergence: Rapid initial progress driven by archive-guided velocity vectors.
  • Diversity: Governed by crowding distance selection of the guide \(x_*\) in the archive.
  • Constraints: Integrated via Deb's feasibility rules during candidate acceptance and archive updates.
  • Non-Convex Fronts: Reaches non-convex fronts effectively through non-dominated archive guidance.
  • Scaling (\(M > 3\)): Velocity update remains \(O(D)\), but archive crowding distance degrades for \(M \ge 5\).
  • Time Complexity: \(O(N \cdot D + N_{arc} \log N_{arc})\) per iteration.

Dedicated Technical Note: What Degrades in NSGA-II at \(M = 5\)?

While NSGA-II records a high numerical hypervolume score (HV = 7.4905 ± 0.0278) due to extreme corner exploration, its internal distribution suffers from three well-documented high-dimensional failure modes:

  • Loss of Selection Pressure (Dominance Resistance):
  • In \(M = 5\) dimensions, the fraction of mutually non-dominated individuals in a random initial population approaches \(100\%\). Almost all individuals occupy Front 1 (\(F_1\)).
  • Because Pareto dominance cannot differentiate between solutions, the entire selection burden falls onto the diversity operator (crowding distance).
  • Boundary Bias of Crowding Distance:
  • Crowding distance assigns \(\infty\) to the boundary extremes along each of the \(M\) axes. In 5 dimensions, there are at least \(2M = 10\) extreme solutions that permanently monopolize survival slots.
  • Solutions on the interior spherical manifold have finite, small crowding distances and are aggressively discarded during \((N+N)\) environmental truncation.
  • Curse of Dimensionality on Perimeter Projections:
  • Crowding distance estimates local density by summing 1D distance differences \((f_m(i+1) - f_m(i-1))\). In 5D hyperspace, two points can be close in 1D projections while being distant in 5D Euclidean space, corrupting true density estimation.
  • Superiority of Structured Reference Approaches (NSGA-III & RVEA):
  • NSGA-III replaces crowding distance with Das & Dennis structured reference points, associating each candidate with its closest reference ray via perpendicular distance (\(d^{\perp}\)). This forces the population to distribute evenly across all sectors of the 5D spherical shell.
  • RVEA employs the Angle-Penalized Distance (\(APD\)), which dynamically scales the penalty between convergence and diversity as \((t/t_{\max})^\alpha\), ensuring robust coverage even without exact hyperplane inversion.

Comprehensive Answers to the 8 Core Examination Questions

Question 1: Why can Weighted Sum never reach a dented part of a non-convex front?

Answer:

In the Weighted Sum method, the scalar subproblem minimizes \(F_w(x) = w_1 f_1(x) + w_2 f_2(x)\) (for \(w_1, w_2 \ge 0, w_1 + w_2 = 1\)). Geometrically, contour lines of \(F_w\) are straight lines of slope \(-w_1 / w_2\). Minimizing \(F_w\) corresponds to translating a supporting hyperplane from \(-\infty\) towards the feasible objective region until it first touches the boundary:

  • If the Pareto front is convex (curves outward towards the ideal point), any boundary point can be made the unique tangent point of contact for some supporting hyperplane slope \(-w_1/w_2\).
  • If the Pareto front is non-convex / concave (dented inward), any supporting hyperplane touching an interior dented point must cut through other feasible points on the flanks. Therefore, the minimum of the linear function along that direction will always occur at one of the outer extremities or boundary vertices, never inside the dent.
  • Mathematical consequence: No non-negative weight vector \((w_1, w_2)\) exists whose minimum is situated inside the dented curve. Sweeping weights causes the solution to jump discontinuously from one end of the dent to the other (as demonstrated on Problem P3, ZDT2).

Question 2: Why did VEGA collapse towards the extremes in your run?

Answer:

VEGA (Vector Evaluated Genetic Algorithm) partitions the population of size \(N\) into \(M\) sub-populations of size \(N/M\). Each sub-population \(m\) is selected based exclusively on objective \(f_m\):

  • Individuals that excel in objective 1 (regardless of how poor they are in objective 2) dominate sub-population 1. Similarly, individuals that excel in objective 2 dominate sub-population 2.
  • When the sub-populations are pooled and shuffled for crossover, the parents represent opposing single-objective extrema.
  • Because selection pressure is exerted purely in the coordinate axes directions, solutions that offer balanced compromises (moderate in both objectives) are never among the best in either sub-population and are systematically filtered out.
  • Over successive generations, this creates strong speciation towards the boundary extrema, leaving the intermediate compromise region completely unpopulated (verified on P2 and P3 in Task A).

Question 3: What does elitism guarantee, and which of your algorithms have it?

Answer:

  • What Elitism Guarantees: Elitism guarantees monotonic non-deterioration of the Pareto-optimal set. Mathematically, if an elite archive or \((N+N)\) selection model is used, a non-dominated solution cannot be displaced or replaced by a strictly inferior or dominated solution. This prevents the evolutionary population from losing high-quality discoveries due to stochastic genetic drift or destructive crossover/mutation.
  • Algorithms with Elitism:
  • Generational \((N+N)\) Evolutionary Elitism: NSGA-II (14), NSGA-III (15), Constrained NSGA-II (21), MODE (27), MO-TLBO (28), MOMA (30), RVEA (31), IBEA (32), HypE (34), R-NSGA-II (35).
  • External Archive-based Elitism: SPEA2 (16), PESA-II (17), MOEA/D (18), MOEA/D-DE (19), MOEA/D-ACO (20), MOPSO (22), MOGWO (23), MOABC (24), MOACO (25), MOSA (26), MOBA (29), SMS-EMOA (33).
  • Non-Elitist Algorithms: VEGA (10), MOGA (11), NSGA (12), NPGA (13) replace parents with offspring without archiving, meaning solutions can drift or be lost.

Question 4: Why does crowding distance stop working well with many objectives, and what replaces it?

Answer:

  • Failure of Crowding Distance in High Dimensions (\(M \ge 5\)):
  • Dominance Resistance: In \(M \ge 5\) dimensions, the probability that two random solutions dominate each other approaches zero. Almost 100% of the population becomes mutually non-dominated (Front 1).
  • Boundary Distortion: Crowding distance assigns \(\infty\) to the extreme points along each objective axis. In \(M\) dimensions, there are at least \(2M\) extreme boundary points. As \(M\) increases, these boundary points consume a dominant fraction of the population, leaving interior trade-off solutions with negligible survival priority.
  • Sparsity in Hyper-volume: Points become sparse in high-dimensional space, and 1D perimeter projections (summing \(\frac{f_m(i+1) - f_m(i-1)}{f_m^{\max} - f_m^{\min}}\)) no longer accurately measure local hyper-spatial density.
  • What Replaces It:
  • Structured Reference Points / Directions: Used in NSGA-III (Das & Dennis simplex lattice) and RVEA (unit reference vectors). Solutions are projected perpendicular to reference lines, preserving uniform distribution across the manifold.
  • Sample-Shared Hypervolume Contributions: Used in HypE and SMS-EMOA, measuring exact or Monte Carlo estimated hypervolume contributions rather than linear distances.

  • In R-NSGA-II, placing \(z^{ref}\) far outside the achievable region distorts the local search, creating clustering at boundary edges and discarding globally efficient trade-offs.

Question 6: Explain Deb's three constraint rules with one numerical example.

Answer:

Deb's Constrained-Dominance Rules:

A solution \(A\) constrained-dominates solution \(B\) if:

  • Solution \(A\) is feasible (\(CV_A = 0\)) and Solution \(B\) is infeasible (\(CV_B > 0\)).
  • Both \(A\) and \(B\) are infeasible, but \(A\) has smaller total constraint violation: \(CV_A < CV_B\).
  • Both \(A\) and \(B\) are feasible, and \(A\) Pareto-dominates \(B\) in objective space: \(\forall m, f_m(A) \le f_m(B)\) and \(\exists m, f_m(A) < f_m(B)\).

Numerical Example (Cantilever Beam Design, Problem P5):

Consider four candidate designs:

  • Point \(A\): \((b=0.02, h=0.06) \implies f = [9.42\text{ kg}, 0.0093\text{ m}]\), \(b h^2 = 7.2 \times 10^{-5} \ge 2.4 \times 10^{-5} \implies \mathbf{CV_A = 0.0}\) (Feasible).
  • Point \(B\): \((b=0.01, h=0.05) \implies f = [3.925\text{ kg}, 0.0160\text{ m}]\), \(b h^2 = 2.5 \times 10^{-5} \ge 2.4 \times 10^{-5} \implies \mathbf{CV_B = 0.0}\) (Feasible).
  • Point \(C\): \((b=0.01, h=0.03) \implies f = [2.355\text{ kg}, 0.0740\text{ m}]\), \(b h^2 = 9.0 \times 10^{-6} < 2.4 \times 10^{-5} \implies \mathbf{CV_C = 0.625}\) (Infeasible).
  • Point \(D\): \((b=0.01, h=0.02) \implies f = [1.570\text{ kg}, 0.2500\text{ m}]\), \(b h^2 = 4.0 \times 10^{-6} < 2.4 \times 10^{-5} \implies \mathbf{CV_D = 0.833}\) (Infeasible).

Rule Application:

  • Rule 1 Check: Compare \(A\) (feasible, mass 9.42) vs \(C\) (infeasible, mass 2.355). Even though \(C\) has much lower mass, \(A\) strictly dominates \(C\) because \(A\) is feasible and \(C\) violates stress limits.
  • Rule 2 Check: Compare \(C\) (\(CV = 0.625\)) vs \(D\) (\(CV = 0.833\)). Both are infeasible, but \(C\) strictly dominates \(D\) because \(CV_C < CV_D\).
  • Rule 3 Check: Compare \(A\) vs \(B\). Both are feasible: \(B\) has lower mass (3.925 < 9.42) while \(A\) has lower deflection (0.0093 < 0.016). Neither Pareto-dominates the other; they are mutually non-dominated trade-offs.

Question 7: How would you detect that your algorithm has prematurely converged?

Answer:

Premature convergence can be detected through multiple quantitative signals monitored across generations:

  • Hypervolume Stagnation: The Hypervolume (HV) curve plateaus early (\(d(\text{HV})/dt \approx 0\)) at a value significantly below the known theoretical ceiling or baseline.
  • Genotypic & Phenotypic Variance Collapse: The population standard deviation in decision space (\(\sigma_x \to 0\)) drops to machine precision, meaning all individuals in the population have collapsed into an identical clone.
  • Severe Spacing Metric Spikes: The Spacing metric \(S\) drops to zero (if all points collapse to a single point) or blows up to large values as intermediate solutions vanish and only isolated clusters remain.
  • Dominance Replacement Ratio: In algorithms like MODE or MOEA/D, the percentage of newly generated offspring that successfully replace their targets drops to zero across multiple consecutive generations.
  • IGD Divergence from Reference: On benchmarks with known Pareto fronts, the Inverted Generational Distance (IGD) remains high and fails to decrease as generation count increases.

Question 8: Why do TOPSIS and VIKOR not count as optimizers?

Answer:

  • Definition of an Optimizer: An optimizer explores, searches, and generates new candidate decision vectors \(x\) across a continuous or discrete decision space \(\mathcal{X}\) to minimize or maximize objective functions subject to constraints.
  • Role of TOPSIS and VIKOR: TOPSIS and VIKOR are Multi-Criteria Decision Making (MCDM) Ranking Methods, not optimizers:
  • Finite Candidate Set: They require a pre-existing, static, finite table of alternatives (e.g., 5 suppliers in P12, or the 100 solutions of an already converged Pareto front).
  • No Generative Exploration: They possess no variation operators (no crossover, mutation, gradient descent, or neighborhood moves) and cannot synthesize new solutions.
  • Post-Processing Function: Their sole mathematical function is to sort and order an existing set of candidate rows based on geometric distance (TOPSIS) or \(L_p\) compromise ranking (VIKOR).
